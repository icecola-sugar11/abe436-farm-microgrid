Project abstract

Abstract

Goals and objectives. This project designs a grid-connected wind–solar–battery system for a specific case-study farm: a 1,000-acre grain farm in Champaign County, IL (40.00°N, 88.20°W) on a 50/50 corn–soybean rotation, with a farmhouse, machine shop, on-farm grain storage, and a high-temperature column grain dryer. At 2024 Illinois yields (217 bu/ac corn, 64 bu/ac soybeans; USDA NASS), the farm harvests 108,500 bu of corn and 32,000 bu of soybeans. Its annual electricity demand is 39.8 MWh, with a harvest-season peak driven by a 31.7 kW dryer load. The objectives are to size the PV array, small wind turbine, and battery to meet this load at the lowest levelized cost of energy (LCOE) and the highest renewable fraction.

Significance. Farms have a strongly seasonal load that most building-scale renewable studies ignore. Measured data for central Illinois show that solar and wind are complementary: solar radiation peaks in summer, while wind is strongest in winter and spring. Quantifying how this complementarity reduces storage needs can help Midwest farmers lower energy costs, cut emissions, and keep critical loads such as grain aeration running during outages.

Methodology. Site-specific solar (Global Solar Atlas, NREL SAM) and wind data (Global Wind Atlas, Weibull analysis, wind-shear correction) are combined with a manufacturer turbine power curve and an hourly energy balance to size a lithium iron phosphate battery. PV-only, wind-only, and hybrid configurations are compared, plus an off-grid case, followed by a 25-year economic analysis.

Expected outcomes. An hourly load profile for the farm, recommended PV, wind, and battery capacities, an equipment list, calculated capacity factors and annual energy production, and the system's economic performance, including how much the hybrid design reduces the battery size needed.

Data sources and calculations

Where the data came from

Wind · Global Wind Atlas 4.0

  2. Placed a point marker on open farmland between Tolono and Philo.
  3. Switched the height to 10 m, 50 m and 100 m and recorded mean wind speed and mean power density at each.
  4. Read the wind roses: prevailing wind is from the south and south-southwest.
Solar · Global Solar Atlas v2.13
  1. Opened globalsolaratlas.info and searched 40.00, −88.20.
  2. Recorded PVOUT (specific PV output), GHI, DNI, DIF, optimum tilt (OPTA) and GTI at that tilt, all per year.
  3. Monthly seasonality taken from a published NREL PVWatts report for Champaign, about 10 km north.
Farm load · extension and federal data

  2. Farm utility cost per acre: farmdoc FBFM table for central Illinois high-productivity farmland.

Equipment and cost
  1. Turbine power curve: Bergey Excel 15 from the NREL Turbine Archive.

Course methods · ABE 436 lectures
  1. Lecture 1: unit conversions (1 hp = 746 W).
  2. Lectures 2–3: tilt angle rule of thumb (tilt ≈ latitude for year-round output).
  3. Lecture 7: PV capacity factor CF = annual energy / (PR × 8,760 h).
  4. Lecture 9: P = ½ρAv³, increase of wind speed with height (power law), Rayleigh distribution.
  5. Lecture 10: wind capacity factor and the approximation CF ≈ 0.087V̄ − PR/D².
  6. Lecture 16: loan annual payment A = P·i(1+i)n/((1+i)n − 1), net savings and payback.
  7. Lab 5: BEM rotor analysis in QBlade.

Calculation steps

1. Annual electricity demand

\[ \begin{aligned} E_{\text{farm}} &= \frac{\$5/\text{acre} \times 1{,}000\ \text{acre}}{\$0.1587/\text{kWh}} = 31{,}506\ \text{kWh/yr}\\[4pt] E_{\text{house}} &= 693\ \text{kWh/month} \times 12 = 8{,}316\ \text{kWh/yr}\\[4pt] E_{\text{total}} &= 31{,}506 + 8{,}316 = 39{,}822\ \text{kWh/yr} \approx \mathbf{39.8\ MWh/yr} \end{aligned} \]

Assumes the FBFM utilities cost is all electricity, priced at the Illinois 2024 residential average.

2. Harvest load: grain dryer and aeration

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Corn} &= 500\ \text{acre} \times 217\ \text{bu/acre} = 108{,}500\ \text{bu}\\ \text{Soybeans} &= 500\ \text{acre} \times 64\ \text{bu/acre} = 32{,}000\ \text{bu}\\[6pt] P_{\text{dryer}} &= 42.5\ \text{hp} \times 0.746\ \tfrac{\text{kW}}{\text{hp}} = \mathbf{31.7\ kW} \qquad \text{(Lecture 1)}\\[4pt] t_{\text{dryer}} &= \frac{108{,}500\ \text{bu}}{300\ \text{bu/h}} = 362\ \text{h}\\[4pt] E_{\text{dryer}} &= 31.7\ \text{kW} \times 362\ \text{h} = 11{,}467\ \text{kWh/yr} \quad (0.106\ \text{kWh/bu})\\[4pt] P_{\text{aeration}} &= 140{,}500\ \text{bu} \times \frac{0.02\ \text{hp}}{1{,}000\ \text{bu}} \times 0.746 = 2.1\ \text{kW} \end{aligned} \]

3. Solar capacity factor and PV size

\[ \begin{aligned} CF_{PV} &= \frac{E_{\text{annual}}}{P_R \times 8{,}760\ \text{h}} = \frac{1{,}440.4}{8{,}760} = \mathbf{16.4\%} \qquad \text{(Lecture 7)}\\[6pt] P_{PV} &= \frac{E_{\text{total}}}{PVOUT} = \frac{39{,}822\ \text{kWh}}{1{,}440.4\ \text{kWh/kWp}} = \mathbf{27.6\ kWp} \end{aligned} \]

Tilt check: the Lectures 2–3 rule gives tilt ≈ latitude = 40°; the Global Solar Atlas optimum for this site is 34°, which we use.

4. Wind speed at hub height (wind shear)

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{V}{V_0} &= \left(\frac{H}{H_0}\right)^{\alpha} \qquad \text{(Lecture 9)}\\[6pt] \alpha &= \frac{\ln(V_{50}/V_{10})}{\ln(50/10)} = \frac{\ln(6.05/4.17)}{\ln 5} = 0.231\\[6pt] V_{30} &= 4.17 \times \left(\frac{30}{10}\right)^{0.231} = \mathbf{5.38\ m/s} \end{aligned} \]

Check: the 50–100 m pair gives α = 0.265. The 10–50 m pair is used because the turbine sits at 30 m.

5. Weibull parameters from the atlas data

\[ \bar{v} = A\,\Gamma\!\left(1 + \frac{1}{k}\right), \qquad \frac{P}{A_{\text{swept}}} = \frac{1}{2}\rho A^{3}\,\Gamma\!\left(1 + \frac{3}{k}\right), \qquad \rho = 1.225\ \text{kg/m}^3 \]

Solving both equations at each height:

HeightMean speedPower densitykA
10 m4.17 m/s86 W/m²1.974.70 m/s
50 m6.05 m/s224 W/m²2.356.83 m/s
30 m (hub)5.38 m/s–2.16 (interpolated)6.07 m/s
\[ A_{30} = \frac{\bar{v}_{30}}{\Gamma(1 + 1/k)} = \frac{5.38}{\Gamma(1 + 1/2.16)} = 6.07\ \text{m/s} \]

k ≈ 2 at 10 m matches the Rayleigh assumption used in lecture.

6. Wind turbine energy and capacity factor

\[ \begin{aligned} f(v) &= \frac{k}{A}\left(\frac{v}{A}\right)^{k-1} e^{-(v/A)^{k}}\\[6pt] AEP &= 8{,}760\ \text{h} \int_{0}^{\infty} P(v)\, f(v)\, dv = \mathbf{34{,}495\ kWh/yr} \quad \text{(Bergey Excel 15)}\\[6pt] CF &= \frac{34{,}495}{15.6\ \text{kW} \times 8{,}760\ \text{h}} = \mathbf{25.2\%} \qquad \text{(Lecture 10)} \end{aligned} \]

Cross-checks with the lecture methods:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Rayleigh, 1 m/s bins (Lecture 9):}\quad & AEP = 35.5\ \text{MWh/yr},\quad CF = 26.0\%\\[6pt] \text{Approximation (Lecture 10):}\quad & CF \approx 0.087\,\bar{V} - \frac{P_R}{D^2} = 0.087(5.38) - \frac{15.6}{9.6^2} = 29.9\% \end{aligned} \]

The Weibull result is used. The quick approximation is higher because it ignores this turbine's power curve.

7. Loan payment and cost of energy (preliminary)

\[ A = P\,\frac{i(1+i)^{n}}{(1+i)^{n} - 1} \qquad \text{(Lecture 16)} \]

With i = 6.25% (USDA FSA direct farm ownership rate, Nov. 2026) and n = 20 years. Installed costs: PV $3.15/W and PV + storage $5.19/W (NREL Q1 2024 cost benchmarks); small wind $7,370/kW (PNNL Distributed Wind Market Report 2024).

\[ CRF = \frac{0.0625\,(1.0625)^{20}}{(1.0625)^{20} - 1} = 0.0890 \]

PV-only

\[ \begin{aligned} P &= 27.6\ \text{kW} \times \$3{,}150/\text{kW} = \$86{,}940\\ A &= \$86{,}940 \times 0.0890 = \$7{,}734/\text{yr}\\ \text{COE} &= \frac{\$7{,}734/\text{yr}}{39{,}822\ \text{kWh/yr}} = \mathbf{19.4\ \text{¢/kWh}} \end{aligned} \]

Wind

\[ \begin{aligned} P &= 15.6\ \text{kW} \times \$7{,}370/\text{kW} = \$114{,}972\\ A &= \$114{,}972 \times 0.0890 = \$10{,}228/\text{yr}\\ \text{COE} &= \frac{\$10{,}228/\text{yr}}{34{,}495\ \text{kWh/yr}} = \mathbf{29.7\ \text{¢/kWh}} \end{aligned} \]

Battery (about one day of farmhouse use: 8,316 ÷ 365 = 22.8 kWh, so 25 kWh)

\[ \begin{aligned} c_{\text{batt}} &= \frac{(\$5.19 - \$3.15)/\text{W} \times 8{,}000\ \text{W}}{13.5\ \text{kWh}} = \$1{,}209/\text{kWh}\\ P_{\text{batt}} &= 25\ \text{kWh} \times \$1{,}209/\text{kWh} \approx \$30{,}200 \end{aligned} \]

Simple payback against the grid price of 15.87 ¢/kWh (Lecture 16):

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Payback} &= \frac{\text{installed cost}}{\text{annual bill savings}}\\[6pt] \text{PV-only:}\ & \frac{\$86{,}940}{39{,}822 \times \$0.1587} = \frac{\$86{,}940}{\$6{,}320/\text{yr}} = \mathbf{13.8\ years}\\[6pt] \text{Wind:}\ & \frac{\$114{,}972}{34{,}495 \times \$0.1587} = \frac{\$114{,}972}{\$5{,}474/\text{yr}} = \mathbf{21.0\ years} \end{aligned} \]

These figures include loan payments only. O&M, equity return, tax credits and grants (such as USDA REAP) will be added in the final economic analysis, and the hybrid mix will be optimized in steps 4–6 of the methodology.

For review

Methodology

The work runs in six steps. Steps 2 and 3 can run in parallel once the farm load from step 1 is fixed; steps 4 to 6 depend on all three.

1

Farm load profile

Build an hourly (8,760 h) electricity profile from the components in the site table.

  • Farmhouse: 8.3 MWh/yr shaped with a typical Illinois residential hourly load profile.
  • Grain dryer: 31.7 kW, run in daytime shifts across the harvest window (late September to early November) until 108,500 bu is dried.
  • Aeration fans (2.1 kW) during cooling cycles after harvest and in spring.
  • Shop, lighting and grain handling: the rest of the 31.5 MWh farm total, spread over working hours.
2

Solar PV modeling

Model a fixed, south-facing array at the optimum 34° tilt in NREL SAM with hourly NSRDB weather data, and check annual output against the Global Solar Atlas yield of 1,440 kWh/kWp.

\[ CF = \frac{E_{\text{annual}}}{P_R \times 8{,}760\ \text{h}} = \frac{1{,}440.4}{8{,}760} = 16.4\% \]
3

Wind resource and turbine output

Correct Global Wind Atlas speeds to hub height, fit a Weibull distribution, and integrate it against the turbine power curve. Use the BEM method from Lab 5 to check rotor Cp at the site's low wind speeds.

\[ v(H) = v_{10}\left(\frac{H}{10}\right)^{\alpha}, \qquad \alpha = \frac{\ln(6.05/4.17)}{\ln 5} = 0.231 \]
\[ f(v) = \frac{k}{A}\left(\frac{v}{A}\right)^{k-1} e^{-(v/A)^{k}} \]
\[ AEP = 8{,}760\ \text{h} \int_0^{\infty} P(v)\,f(v)\,dv = 34.5\ \text{MWh/yr at 30 m} \]

Hub heights of 24, 30 and 37 m will be compared (30.7, 34.5 and 38.3 MWh/yr gross).

4

Hourly energy balance and battery sizing

For every hour, compute net load = demand − PV − wind. Surplus charges an LFP battery (90% usable depth of discharge); remaining surplus is exported, and deficits are covered by the battery and then the grid. Battery size is chosen to minimize grid imports per dollar spent, and to cover one day of critical load in the off-grid case.

5

Configuration comparison

Run the balance for PV-only (≈27.6 kWp to match annual demand), wind-only (one 15.6 kW turbine), and hybrid mixes, each with and without a battery, plus one off-grid case. Compare renewable fraction, grid imports, exports and storage required. Tools: HOMER Pro if available, otherwise SAM with Excel or Python.

6

Economic analysis and sensitivity

Over 25 years, calculate installed cost from NREL cost benchmarks and manufacturer quotes, O&M, LCOE, NPV and simple payback, using Ameren Illinois rates. Test sensitivity to electricity price, equipment cost, wind speed and load.

\[ LCOE = \frac{CAPEX + \sum_{t=1}^{25} \dfrac{O\&M_t}{(1+r)^t}}{\sum_{t=1}^{25} \dfrac{E_t}{(1+r)^t}} \]
